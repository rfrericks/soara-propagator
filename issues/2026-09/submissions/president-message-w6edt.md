# President's Message — September 2026

SOARA held its annual summer picnic on August 22 at Laguna Niguel Regional Park. It
was a warmish day, but there was a good breeze and we stayed comfortable under the
picnic shelter. I want to thank all of the SOARA members and their families who
came out to enjoy a day in the park. If you missed the picnic this year, I hope you
are able to attend in 2027.

September is National Preparedness Month and I want to talk a little bit about what
that means in the context of our hobby. Amateur radio is the only means of instant
communications where both ends of the conversation do not need any external
resources to make it happen. We can run our stations on batteries, use a portable
antenna and generally set up anywhere to provide communications services. HF
communications can directly span longer distances to places outside the immediate
disaster area, which no other service can reliably provide. So, it would seem that
we might plan to be called upon to provide those services for local governments and
agencies in the case of a large scale disaster. Out here in California, that would
likely be an earthquake or perhaps a tsunami. While that might be one possibility
for using our skill and Amateur radio license to help, there are many others,
including Health and Welfare traffic from your neighbors. Don't underestimate the
value of being able to provide a way for your neighbors to tell their families in
other states they are OK, when all cell service is disrupted and the internet is
down for a week or more.

However, take a step back and ask yourself, am I and my family capable of being
self-sufficient for a week or more? After all, you can't help others until you and
everyone around you are safe, have food and water, medicines and some shelter from
the elements. Starting from a position of safety, you can then go help others.
RACES/ARES train that the first step after any disaster occurs is to make sure you
and your family are safe before considering deployment to help others.

There are many checklists and other guides to preparing for various scenarios. I'd
suggest looking at resources from ARRL or FEMA to get a start on making your own
list. You should have a set of supplies for your home that will sustain you for at
least a week minimum, 2 weeks would be better. You should also have a kit for one
of your cars and keep that car's fuel tank or battery at least half full at all
times. If you often travel a significant distance from home for work, plan to have
some supplies in a second car if you get stranded away from home, with the primary
vehicle at home available to leave if the disaster places it at risk. I am probably
repeating many things you have heard before, but if you have not actually made a
plan, now is the time to do that.

Our meeting in September will have several local ham groups that will talk about
how they drill for emergencies and what kinds of other activities they do to help
the community. The best way to be prepared is to train and plan with one of those
groups. I hope you will be able to attend the September meeting and hear what these
groups have to say.

On another topic, I wanted to mention something that happened recently. While we
were conducting one of our Tuesday night VHF/UHF nets, a participant had to
interrupt his transmission because a LiMnO pack had caught fire and was making some
smoke and heat. He used a fire extinguisher to try and put out the fire, which may
have self extinguished. It turns out that the fire extinguisher would have been
helpful to put out the fire on/in other objects ignited by the battery, but the
extinguisher would have little effect on the lithium battery. Battery fires are
very hard to put out because they are often sealed units armored from direct
contact with extinguishing agents and they generate massive quantities of their own
heat. If you have a battery fire, especially a lithium battery fire, the best way
to make it safe is to place the battery in a metal bucket filled with fine sand and
then carry it outside. The sand may partially smother the flames and will also
insulate the surroundings from the heat.

I had a similar problem with a very small lithium battery (800mAh) that started to
heat up and eventually melted the wires going to the device, all while laying in my
hand. I suspect the supply leads shorted, but I got a nice burned strip on my hand
before I dropped it in a bucket.

The take-away here is that if you are working with batteries, have a plan about
what you are going to do if the pack or wiring catches fire. A metal bucket with
sand is one idea. A non-conductive non-flammable surface is another. Either way you
want the whole mess outside and well away from the house and other important
objects.

One final reminder. SOARA dues are due on October 1st of each year. We will be
sending out a notice via email to all members. Thanks for your continued support of
SOARA.

Stay safe and I look forward to hearing you on the air or seeing you at a meeting.
